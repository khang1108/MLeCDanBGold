\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Multi-event video retrieval uses descriptions of ordered moments to find a video and localize the described events.
In the Ho Chi Minh City AI Challenge, Known-Item Search (KIS) queries can describe several moments while requesting a single frame; Temporal Retrieval and Alignment of Key Events (TRAKE) explicitly requests an ordered sequence of event frames.
Recent SoICT systems combine multimodal evidence with temporal search, including dynamic programming (DP), beam search, and coherence scoring~\cite{luu2025dante,do2025tars,vu2025madtempo,nguyen2025cscs,dinh2025kpter,nguyen2025agentdp,ho2025lucifertrace,nguyen2025eventsspeak}.
Competition-level success, however, does not by itself isolate the quality of an autonomous first pass from subsequent interaction, or reveal whether every described event was localized correctly.
For multi-moment KIS queries, auditing all events therefore goes beyond the competition's single-frame output.

We distinguish two failure modes.
The first pass can return the wrong video, in which case no amount of temporal reasoning inside that video helps.
Or it can return the right video but place one or more events at the wrong moment, which a single-frame submission cannot fully expose.
The first failure requires reconsidering the video choice, for example through query reformulation or candidate inspection; the second may be addressed by repairing individual event placements.

We study these failures in an existing training-free pipeline, asking three questions: where does its first pass fail, can its ranking scores reveal a wrong video, and which components help repair event placements under a small action budget?
Our contribution is an empirical analysis of reliability and correction, using established score-based predictors and constrained DP rather than a new retrieval model.
Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{An event-level evaluation set and audit.} A frozen set of 40 queries (113 events) separates video ranking from within-video alignment; only 25\% of queries are fully correct even with the correct video given (Sects.~\ref{sec:benchmark}--\ref{sec:diagnosis}).
\item \textbf{A study of first-pass failure signals.} On 70 queries, score margins discriminate correct from wrong videos, with held-out AUROCs of 0.78--0.85 when the signal is selected on training sets. We distinguish this discrimination from the reliability of a warning threshold (Sect.~\ref{sec:failure_prediction}).
\item \textbf{A controlled oracle-correction study.} We compare starting paths, order constraints, proposals, inspection order, and propagation. With the correct video, five actions raise whole-sequence correctness from 25\% to 70\%; most of the decoder advantage comes from initialization and ordering, rather than propagation (Sect.~\ref{sec:correction}).
\end{enumerate}

Together, these results motivate a workflow that warns about an uncertain video choice and supports local repair after the video is confirmed.
We evaluate the two mechanisms separately; their combination and its benefit to human users remain to be tested.
